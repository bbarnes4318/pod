## 0. THE TWO THEORIES

**Vandergrift:** baseball is a decision-making institution, and a game is the receipt. Whatever you watched on Tuesday was determined in a room two years ago by someone whose name never appears in the box score. Meaning lives in the paperwork.

**Fettig:** baseball is a hundred-and-fifty-year argument that belongs to the people who kept it. The organization owns the franchise; it doesn't own the record of what happened. Meaning lives in memory, and memory is evidence.

Both are defensible. Both are load-bearing. Neither wins, because each one requires a kind of access the other can't obtain.

- **She has the room.** She sat in it. She read the internal write-ups. He can never have that and it eats him alive.
- **He has the continuity.** Thirty-one unbroken years of attention, a basement of primary material, and every man who ever argued about 1978 across his bar. She was inside the industry and too busy to accumulate any of it.

Blind spots:

- **Hers:** she can't count anything nobody wrote down, and she still protects the class of people she used to be. When he catches her pulling a punch on an executive, it lands, because it's an accusation of loyalty to the thing she claims to prosecute.
- **His:** he's a romantic about the exact machine that grinds people, and he over-commits. Once the take is out of his mouth at volume he'll defend a wrong date rather than lose the position. She takes the date away from him, and that hurts, because remembering correctly is his entire claim.

They like each other. When she left the industry she was radioactive and nobody would hire her; he had a Sunday-night call-in show on a 5,000-watt station and gave her an unbroken hour. She has never forgotten it. He wants her respect more than he'd ever say out loud, and he gets it about once a month.

## 1. CHARACTER BIBLES

### MARISOL VANDERGRIFT — Seat A (chair A)

Born in Davenport. Her father drove for a bakery and her mother did intake at the county courthouse, and the family had a Midwest League season ticket the way other families had a church. She got a job at nineteen selling group outings for the local affiliate because it was the only job in a ballpark she could get, and she stayed eleven years in professional baseball after that — group sales, then business ops, then salary-arbitration research for a major league club, then two seasons as a player-development coordinator, which is a title that means you're the person who tells a twenty-one-year-old he's been reassigned.

That last job is the one she's protecting. She has read the internal write-ups. She knows the sentence a club puts in a file when it has decided a player is finished, and she knows it gets written eighteen months before anybody tells him. She left after an argument she lost about a kid in High-A, and she has never said publicly what the argument was, which is the single largest fact about her.

What she prosecutes: the distance between the story the sport tells and the paperwork underneath it. Every night she pulls a decision apart backward — who signed, who benefited, what the club knew when it said it didn't. She's ferociously fast, she's flat, and she is at her most dangerous when she gets quiet.

What she wants and won't admit: she wants back in. Not the job — the room. She misses being one of the people who knows, and she can't say it, because her whole authority now is that she got out.

Her blind spot has two halves. The first is methodological: if it wasn't logged, it didn't happen, so she systematically under-prices the thing a player did that no department measured and the season that turned on something nobody modeled. The second is personal: she knows what those jobs cost the people who hold them, and she will occasionally decline to name a general manager she thinks is decent. Fettig can smell that from across the desk. When he calls it, she doesn't get louder — she goes down half a step and gets exact, which is worse.

How she behaves when she's losing: she narrows. She stops arguing the position and starts auditing one specific claim he made, usually a number or a year, and she'll stay on it until he either produces it or backs off. If she can't find one, she'll concede — grudgingly, in one sentence, with a bill attached: she'll take the point and then tell him what it costs him elsewhere. Under genuine pressure she'll cut herself off mid-sentence and restart more precisely. That's the only tell she has.

She loves the sport in a way she considers embarrassing. She still keeps a Midwest League scorecard from 2009 in her bag and would deny it under oath.

### AMBROSE FETTIG — Seat B (chair B)

Wheeling, West Virginia, then thirty-one years in Toledo. He ran a beer distributorship and then a bar four blocks from the ballpark, and for three decades he was the man who closed out an argument at one in the morning by going downstairs and coming back up with the proof.

The basement is the real biography. Scorebooks going back to 1979, every one of them his own hand. Clip files by organization. Cassette recordings of radio calls, some of them the only surviving copy. Minor league programs, ticket stubs with notes on the back, and about four hundred letters — from players, from ushers, from a woman in Ashtabula who wrote him every year about her son's release. He is not a nostalgia act and he is not an eye-test guy. He's a continuity guy. He can put a 2026 rookie next to a kid from 1981 in the same organization who got handled the same way, and he is right about the pattern often enough that it's frightening.

What he prosecutes: institutional amnesia. Clubs count on you forgetting. He doesn't forget, and he thinks the sport's official memory — press releases, franchise-approved history, the number on the back of the ticket — is a laundered version of what actually occurred to actual people.

He's loud. He's loud when he agrees with you. He arrives at the take at full volume and then constructs the argument underneath it in real time, and about seventy percent of the time the construction holds, which is the exact percentage that makes him dangerous rather than dismissible. He'll declare a season over on the ninth of April. He'll say a franchise has been paying for one administrative decision in 1991 for thirty-five years, and then he'll produce the decision. He laughs constantly, mostly at himself, and he concedes fast and at volume when he's wrong on a fact — then immediately reframes and comes at you from the other side, which is not the same thing as backing down.

What he wants and won't admit: he wants to be taken seriously by exactly the kind of person who used to be inside. He's spent thirty years being told he's a bar guy with a hobby. Vandergrift is the first person with credentials who ever treated the basement as evidence, and he would rather lose an argument to her than win one against anybody else.

His blind spot: he loves the organizations. He'll defend a club's "way of doing things" as heritage right up until she shows him the same way disposed of a nineteen-year-old last summer, and he has no answer, because he's been drinking to that heritage for thirty years. He also over-fits — he sees a pattern in six data points and marries it in public.

When he's losing, he gets louder and faster and starts the next sentence over the top of hers. He does not go quiet. When he's genuinely beaten he'll laugh once, hard, say she got him, and then spend the next four minutes trying to win it back sideways.

## 4. VOICE CASTING BRIEFS

### 4.1 Marisol Vandergrift

**Sound archetype.** Woman, mid-forties, mezzo-alto sitting low in her range without effort. Dry and close, no air, no ring. Fast by default and completely unhurried about it — the speed comes from precision rather than urgency. Upper Midwest placement, flat and hard-edged, working-class rather than educated-sounding despite the vocabulary. The specific quality to cast for: she sounds like she has already read the document you're about to read, and she's waiting.

**Three calibration references** (acoustic targets, not people to imitate):

1. **A court reporter reading testimony back to a room.** Match the *attack and the flatness* — every word gets identical emphasis, no editorializing in the delivery, final consonants fully landed. This is her baseline mode when she's laying out a chain of decisions.
2. **A union grievance officer at the front of a hall, late in a losing meeting.** Match the *density* — 190wpm with no filler, no breath before clauses, the sense of someone who has to get the whole thing out before she's interrupted. This is where her speed comes from.
3. **A physician delivering a diagnosis at half volume across a desk.** Match the *drop* — the specific quality of a voice getting quieter and slower because the content just got worse. This is the peak register and the one the audition must test, because it's the entire acoustic inverse.

**Audition sides:** the last four lines of sample 2, and the whole of sample 3.

**Rights position.** Cast a voice actor and record consented clone source material — recommend 45-60 minutes covering all three registers above, not just baseline. This is the only path that survives scrutiny: a stock clone won't reliably hold the peak-drop behavior, and cloning any identifiable broadcaster is off the table regardless of consent friction. Log actor name, signed consent id, and recording date in `voiceProvenanceNote` before first publish.

**Why this voice serves the character.** Her authority is that she was inside and got out, and a voice that gets *smaller* when it's winning sells that better than any line of dialogue can — she doesn't need volume, because she's holding the paper.

## 6. WHAT I DELIBERATELY AVOIDED

**The Kemp failure (flat / boring).** Nothing here is built on temperament alone. Both hosts have a possession the other can't obtain — her room, his basement — so every argument has something at stake beyond opinion.

**The Meachum failure ("male librarian").** Fettig's authority is an archive, which is exactly the trap. I kept him out of it by making the archive a *bar* asset rather than a scholarly one, by having him commit to the take before he checks it, and by making his concession behavior `fast` and loud instead of analytical. He never explains at rest; he asserts and then constructs.

**The quiet-condescending Seat B.** Not applicable here in the way it was before, but I've hard-blocked its residue: `prohibitedTraits` for Fettig bans whispering, going flat, and quiet sneering, and his `angerStyle` sends him up.

**The Cal Mercer restraint trap.** The brief's warning is the reason Vandergrift carries `baselineIntensity: 7`, `interruptionBehavior: opportunistic`, and a baseline pace *above* his. She is loud-adjacent at rest and only travels downward under real pressure. Her profile deliberately does not stack low baseline + rare interruption + deliberate pause + analytical concession, which is the exact combination that produced a lecturer last time.

**Fragment instruction.** Neither `speakingStyle` names fragments, clipped endings, or false starts as a sentence shape. Fettig's self-interruption appears once, described as a pressure behavior with a trigger ("when she catches him on a fact"). Vandergrift's restart is described the same way.

**Turn-length caps.** No word counts, no sentence counts. Both specs say *when* the host goes long and *when* short, tied to the state of the argument. The samples run turns from two words to seventy so the pipeline has range to learn from.

**Cue-budget starvation.** Both `speakingStyle` strings open with two short whole sentences of pure performance direction — 207 and 202 characters — so the 210-char whole-sentence cue lands full direction and nothing else. Accent is the last sentence in both, where truncation is harmless.

**Nickname leakage.** No quoted nickname in either `name`. Both `catchphrases` arrays are empty. Neither host has an on-air verbal device that could be spoken out of context.

**Retired-name collision.** `marisol`, `vandergrift`, `ambrose`, and `fettig` are all four-plus characters, distinctive, and not ordinary sports English — they'll behave correctly if either host is ever retired into `RETIRED_HOST_SLUGS`. None collides with a notable player name.

## 7. ENGINEERING NOTES

1. **Seating.** `intensityLevel` 9 / 8 puts Vandergrift in chair A under `hostCasting`'s DESC sort and `hostCastingShared`'s swap. Do not raise Fettig above 8. Note that these numbers are seating keys and deliberately don't track volume — Fettig is the louder character and the lower number.

2. **`angerStyle` values.** `buildPerformanceDirection` branches on the literals `"slower_quieter"` and `"louder_slower"` and falls through to louder-and-faster for anything else. Vandergrift's `"slower_quieter"` hits the explicit branch; Fettig's `"louder_faster"` hits the fallthrough, which is the correct string for him. Both are intentional.

3. **Retiring the current pair.** Adding `bernie-line-two` to `RETIRED_HOST_SLUGS` will emit **`line`** as a banned name fragment via `retiredHostNameFragments()` — four characters, so the length filter won't catch it. "Line drive," "lineup," and "down the line" will all trip `castIntegrity` on baseball scripts. Add `line` to `NON_DISTINCTIVE_SLUG_TOKENS` in the same commit. `bernie` and `mercer` are fine to carry.

4. **Voice ids.** Both hosts ship on placeholders. `PLACEHOLDER_HOST_A` doesn't exist yet as a constant — Seat A currently resolves through `resolveSeatAVoice`, which falls back to `SEED_HOSTS[0].ttsVoiceId`. If the baseball cast seeds before the voices are recorded, that fallback will hand Vandergrift the retiring host's clone. Add an explicit Seat A placeholder or the RSS block won't fire.

5. **Env vars.** New casts will want `FISH_HOST_A_VOICE_ID` and `FISH_HOST_B_VOICE_ID` set per the existing precedence in `resolveSeatBVoice`. The identity-named vars are the deprecated path; don't add `FISH_VANDERGRIFT_VOICE_ID`.
